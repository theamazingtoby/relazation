Journaling

Ongoing practice — build capacity over time, not just in a crisis.

  1. Write for 5 to 10 minutes
  2. No editing
  3. No re-reading
  4. Let the thought finish on the page
Why
Externalizing a thought reduces its grip. The brain stops rehearsing what's already been recorded.
Tradition
Expressive writing research shows measurable anxiety reduction from the practice.
When
Racing thoughts at night. Before a big decision. Daily practice.

What it is. Expressive writing — putting thoughts onto a page without editing, without reviewing, without trying to produce anything coherent. Five to ten minutes. The point is not the journal itself; it is the act of completing a thought outside the head. Unwritten looping thoughts get rehearsed by the brain indefinitely. A fully written thought gets filed.

How to do it. Write for five to ten minutes. Do not edit while writing. Do not go back and re-read. Let whatever is running end on the page — if a thought spirals, let it spiral and write it out until it reaches its own conclusion. If nothing comes, write that until something does. When the time is up, close the notebook and put it away. The writing does not need to be kept: some people shred or burn old pages, which removes the self-consciousness about what is recorded and makes the writing freer.

When to use it. Racing thoughts at night. A decision that keeps looping without resolving. The days when a specific anxiety has installed itself and nothing else seems to move it. As a daily practice on ordinary days it functions as a prevention tool, clearing the accumulation before it builds into something that wakes you at 3am.

Mechanism. James Pennebaker's expressive writing research, running from the 1980s to the present, consistently shows that brief periods of written disclosure reduce psychological distress and lower cortisol over time. The working mechanism is that externalizing a thought reduces the cognitive load of maintaining it: the brain stops rehearsing what has already been recorded. For rumination specifically — thoughts that loop because they feel unresolved — writing forces the thought to a conclusion, even a temporary one, which is enough to interrupt the loop. The no-editing rule is essential to the mechanism: editing shifts the task from discharge to performance, which is a different cognitive process with different effects.

Evidence level: strong (Pennebaker's expressive writing research, replicated across multiple populations and contexts over 40 years)
