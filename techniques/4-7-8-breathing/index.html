4-7-8 Breathing

Ongoing practice — build capacity over time, not just in a crisis.

  1. Inhale through the nose for 4 counts
  2. Hold the breath for 7 counts
  3. Exhale through the mouth for 8 counts
Why
The long hold and even-longer exhale push the nervous system toward calm faster than normal breathing.
Tradition
Popularized by Dr. Andrew Weil, adapted from pranayama breath-ratio practices.
When
Before sleep. Racing thoughts at night. Acute anxiety.

What it is. 4-7-8 breathing is a paced breath pattern — inhale for 4 counts, hold for 7, exhale for 8 — popularized by Dr. Andrew Weil, who adapted it from pranayama breath-ratio practices. It combines two separate calming mechanisms in one cycle: a breath hold (like Box Breathing) and a strongly extended exhale (like Extended Exhale, but pushed further), which is why it's often reached for specifically at bedtime rather than as a general-purpose daytime reset.

How to do it. 1. Exhale completely through the mouth, making a soft whoosh sound 2. Close the mouth and inhale quietly through the nose for a count of 4 3. Hold the breath for a count of 7 4. Exhale completely through the mouth for a count of 8, again with a whoosh 5. This is one cycle. Repeat for 4 cycles total when starting out

Weil's own guidance caps the first few weeks of practice at 4 cycles twice a day — the breath hold is a real physiological stimulus, and more isn't automatically better, especially before the body has adapted. Some people feel mildly lightheaded on the first few attempts; this settles with practice and is a sign to slow down or shorten the count (e.g. 4-5-6 instead of 4-7-8), not to push through.

When to use it. Most commonly reached for at bedtime, as a wind-down ritual rather than a mid-day reset — the breath-hold component asks for more stillness and attention than something like the physiological sigh, which is built for the middle of an acute moment. Also useful for racing thoughts after lying down, or as a short pre-sleep routine paired with the rest of an evening wind-down.

Mechanism. The count itself does two things at once. The 7-count hold builds tolerance to rising CO2 — the same mechanism Box Breathing trains — teaching the nervous system that a pause in breathing is survivable, not an emergency. The 8-count exhale, longer relative to the inhale than almost any other common technique on this list, maximizes the parasympathetic shift that Extended Exhale describes: exhale drives vagal tone up, and a longer exhale drives it up further.

Because the ratio (4:7:8, roughly 1:1.75:2) asks more of the breath hold than most beginners are used to, first attempts can produce a brief sense of urgency or lightheadedness — this is the CO2 tolerance threshold being tested, not a sign that something is wrong. It fades within a handful of practice sessions.

Evidence level: widely used and popularized clinically (Dr. Andrew Weil); direct RCT evidence for the specific 4-7-8 ratio is thinner than for box breathing or extended exhale alone, though it draws on the same well-studied breath-hold and extended-exhale mechanisms as those techniques
