Grounding (5-4-3-2-1)

In the moment — for when you're in acute stress or anxiety, right now.

  1. Name 5 things you see
  2. Name 4 things you hear
  3. Name 3 things you feel
  4. Name 2 things you smell
  5. Name 1 thing you taste. Silently is fine.
Why
Directs attention outward through the five senses, interrupting the internal anxious narrative.
Tradition
A standard grounding tool in trauma-informed and CBT practice.
When
Dissociation. Flashback. Sudden anxiety spike.

What it is. A sensory reorientation exercise that directs attention outward through the five senses in sequence: five things seen, four heard, three felt, two smelled, one tasted. The sequence is designed to be worked through fully — actually looking, listening, and noticing — which takes long enough to interrupt whatever internal loop was running. It can be done silently, making it usable in public, in a crowd, or in any situation where other techniques would draw attention.

How to do it. Work through the sequence slowly, naming each item to yourself. Five things you can see — not just the obvious ones, the specific ones: the grain in a surface, the shadow under a chair, a color you hadn't noticed. Four things you can hear — not just background noise, but what is actually arriving. Three things you can physically feel — texture, temperature, weight, the pressure of the floor under your feet. Two things you can smell. One thing you can taste. The act of searching for less obvious details in each sense is part of the technique: it keeps attention genuinely occupied rather than going through the motions.

When to use it. Dissociation. Flashback. A sudden anxiety spike that has detached from the present environment. The technique was developed for exactly these moments — when the nervous system is somewhere else and needs to be walked back into the room. It also works as a reset before a stressful situation, on public transport, or anywhere that an internal loop has been running longer than intended.

Mechanism. Sustained sensory attention directs neural activity toward the sensory cortices and away from the medial prefrontal cortex and default mode network — the network responsible for self-referential thought and rumination. The counting structure prevents partial engagement: it requires genuine contact with the environment rather than a cursory glance. In trauma-informed frameworks, grounding is understood as returning the nervous system to the present moment, interrupting the time-displacement that drives flashback and dissociation.

Evidence level: standard clinical tool in trauma-informed therapy, DBT, and CBT; widely used in PTSD treatment protocols
